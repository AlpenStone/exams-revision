DNA stands for **deoxyribonucleic acid**, it was discovered by James Watson & Francis Crick with data from Rosalind Franklin. 

## Structure of DNA

- DNA is made up of individual nucleotides which consists of a phosphate group, a deoxyribose sugar unit, and a nitrogenous base (Adenine, Thymine, Guanine, Cytosine)
- DNA has a double helix shape
- DNA is divided into several lengths called chromosomes. (Humans have 23)
    - Each chromosome contains lengths of code that determine different characteristics called genes
        - A gene is a unit of inheritance, passed from one generation to the next.


## Reproduction

Reproduction ensure the continuity of organisms by enabling parents to pass their genetic material to their offspring.

There is 2 types of reproduction: asexual and sexual reproduction. Asexual does not require gametes (sex cells) 

### Sexual reproduction
Sexual reproduction consists of haploid sex cells (gametes). They fuse (fertilisation) to form a zygote, which then undergoes division.

### Asexual reproduction
Asexual reproduction consists of a single parent organism that produces offspring that are genetically identical to itself.

Summary:
| Asexual  | Sexual |
| -------- | ------- |
| One parent  | Two parents  |
| No variation (offspring the same) - like a clone | Genetic variation - offspring different but similar |
| Adapting to environment is hard | Allows organisms to adapt |
| Chromosomes are from one parent | Chromosomes: 1/2 mother, 1/2 father |
| Can reproduce quickly: Binary fission, budding, fragmentation, parthenogensis | Slower process: pollination, fertilisation |
|  | Consists of haploid gametes to form a zygote. They fuse (fertilisation) to form a zygote. |

## Cell Replication

### Mitosis
Mitosis is the process of cell division that leads to two identical cells. Our cells undergo this process for growth, repair, and to replace worn out cells.

1. **Interphase**
    - The cell spends most of its time during this phase; it grows and prepares to divide; the cell carries out their normal functions
    - DNA replication takes place during this period; this is a crucial step before the cell can begin mitosis
2. **Prophase**
    - DNA condenses into visible chromosomes - each into two identical sister chromatids
    - Nuclear membrane breaks down, allowing the chromosomes to move freely around the cell
    - Spindle fibers began to form
3. **Metaphase**
    - Chromosomes line up in the middle of the cell
    - Spindle fibers attach to each chromosome at its centrosome
4. **Anaphase**
    - The spindle fibers shorten and pull the chromatids to opposite ends of the cell (poles)
    - Once separated, each chromatid is considered as one chromosome
5. **Telophase**
    - Chromosomes arrive at the 2 poles of the cell and begins to uncoil into chromatin
    - Nuclear membrane forms around each set of chromatin
    - Spindle fibers break down
6. **Cytokinesis**
    - Cytoplasm of cell divides, forming 2 separate daughter cells

> Please note that *Interphase* and *Cytokinesis* is not a part of mitosis

### Meiosis
Meiosis has 2 stages that produces four, genetically varied daughter cells. This process is used to make gametes for sexual reproduction.

#### Meiosis I
1. **Interphase**
    - DNA replicates in the cell.
2. **Prophase I**
    - The chromosomes join up with the pair from the other parent; the mother's pair bind with the father's pair forming a group of chromosomes called homologus chromosomes.
    - One chromatid from each pair gets entangled with a corresponding chromatid from the other side; crossing-over. The two chromatids swap certain sections of DNA (recombination)
3. **Metaphase I**
    - The chromosomes align themselves up in the middle of the cell
4. **Anaphase I**
    - Spindle fibers pull the chromsomes apart to opposite ends of the cell (poles) 
5. **Telophase I & Cytokinesis**
    - Cell pinches apart in the middle
    - Nuclear membrane reforms around the 2 new daughter cells 

#### Meiosis II
6. **Prophase II**
    - Chromatin clumps up to form chromosomes again in both cells
7. **Metaphase II**
    - Chromosomes line up in the middle of both cells
8. **Anaphase II**
    - Chromatids are pulled apart by spindle fibers to opposite ends of both cells
9. **Telophase II & Cytokinesis**
    - The cells pinch in the middle
    - 4 daughter cells are formed

## Alleles
- Alleles are different variations of the same gene.
- Different alleles can lead to different versions of characteristics, called traits
- An allele can be dominant (always expressed) or recessive (needs 2 of the same to be expressed)

**Genotype**: The unobservable genetic blueprint of an individual.
**Phenotype**: The observable, physical traits/characteristics that result from genetic code, influenced by the environment.

### Genetic Variation
Sexual reproduction mixes genetic information in 3 main ways. 

1. **Crossing over**: During meiosis I, homologous chromosomes exchange segments of DNA, forming new allele combinations.
2. **Independent Assortment**: Homologous chromosome pairs line up randomly during metaphase I, so different combinations align end up in gametes.
3. **Random Fertilisation**: Any sperm can fuse with any egg, creating unique combinations of alleles.

Genetic variation can:
- Increase differences between individuals
- Allow populations to adapt to new environments
- Reduces risk that one disease or threat will affect everyone
- Provides raw material for evolution

> Without variation, species are more vulnerable to extinction.

### Inherited Disorders (plus case study - cystic fibrosis)
A genetic disorder is an inherited medical condition. Parents who are heterzygous for these conditions are called **carriers**. If both parents are carriers, there is a 1 in 4 chance that their child will inherit the disorder. 

**Cystic Fibrosis**:
- The CFTR gene provides instructions to make a protein that forms a cholride channel in cell membranes
- A mutation in the CFTR gene causes the body to produce thick, sticky mucus
- Mucus builds up in the lungs, leads to breathing problems and increasing infections

## Mutation
A mutation is a change in the DNA sequence, it can have a neutral (no harm) effect, harmful (disrupts protein function or disease) effect, or beneficial (new advantageous trait) effect.

Types of DNA mutations:
1. Substitution - where a nucleotide is replaced by another nucleotide
2. Insertion - an extra nucleotide is added into the DNA sequence
3. Deletion - a nucleotide is removed from the DNA sequence

After a mutation, there could be 1 of 3 outcomes:
1. No effect (silent) - normal protein function 
2. Altered protein - protein structure changes; protein function may be changed
3. Non-functional protein - protein cannot work properly; protein function lost

Somatic mutations occur in body cells; cannot be passed to offspring.
Germline mutations occur in gametes; can be passed to offspring.

> Mutations can create new alleles and increase genetic variation in a population.

## Radiation

### Types of Radiation

There are 3 types of radiation: gamma, beta, and alpha.

#### Alpha Radiation
- Made up of 2 protons and neutrons; same structure as a Helium nucleus
- Elements that commonly emit alpha particles - they are quite large and releasing an alpha particle helps them become more stable:
    - Uranium
    - Thorium
    - Radium
    - Polonium
- Because alpha particles are relatively strong and heavy, they have strong ionising ability but travel short distances (stopped by paper or skin)
- Have low penetration power
- If inhaled, it can cause serious damage to nearby cells as they release large amounts of energy over a short distance, which can damage DNA, cause mutations etc.

##### Case study: Radon
Radon is a radioactive gas that is produced naturally by the decay of uranium and thorium in rocks and soil.

In Ireland, many parts of the country has high levels of uranium in its rocks and soil, leading to high levels of radon gas in the air. Radon gas can build up indoors, increasing the risk of lung cancer.

Because radon release alpha particles when inhaled, it can cause siginificant damage to lung tissue as the alpha particles are released close to cells. This can increase mutation rates and cancer risk over time.

##### Uses of alpha radiation

Smoke detectors use Americium-241 which emits alpha particles. These particles ionise the air, allowing a current to flow between two electrodes. If smoke enters the detector, the alpha particles are blocked by the smoke particles (absorbes alpha particles), reduces ionisation and breaks the current, thus triggering the alarm. 

#### Beta Radiation
- Beta particles are fast moving electrons
- Beta particles are emitted from unstable nuclei as atoms try to achieve stability
- Beta particles are smaller and lighter than alpha particles, so they have less ionising ability but can travel further
- During beta decay, a neutron turns into a proton and emits an electron (beta particle) in order to become more stable. The atomic number increase by one and the atom turns into a new element.

##### Properties of Beta Particles
- Beta particles are small, fast moving particles with a charge of -1
- Moderate ionising power and penetration power (stopped by thin sheet of aluminium)
- Beta particle can damage living tissue and affect DNA

##### Case study - Iodine-131
Iodine-131 is a radioactive isotope of iodine. Iodine is naturally taken up by the thyroid gland, which means it can accumlate in the thryoid tissue. The radiation ionises and breaks chemical bonds, which can lead to mutations and DNA damage. If the damage is not repaired, it can increase the risk of thyroid cancer.   

Chernobyl accident release 131I, and led to increased incidents of thyroid cancer especially in children.

##### Uses of Iodine-131
Doctors use iodine-131 to treat overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism) or thyroid cancer.

Because iodine accumulates in the thyroid gland, the radiation from the iodine-131 can target and destroy harmufl cells without affecting much of the surrounding tissue.


#### Gamma Radiation
- Gamma rays are high energy electromagnetic waves; no mass, no charge
- It is often released at the same time as alpha or beta decay, when the nucleus loses excess energy.

##### Properties of Gamma Rays
- Low ionising but high penetration power
- Can pass through human body; only stopped by thick materials such as lead or concrete
- Because it can travel long distances and penetrate deeply, it can cause damage to the body
- It can ionise atoms in the body, leading to mutations, errors during cell division, and an increased risk of cancer
- It can damage cells from the outside

##### Case Study - Hiroshima & Nagasaki
High levels of radiation of gamma radiation cause radiation sickness, which include bone marrow damage (affecting blood cell production), damage to digestive system, and weak immune system. 

This happened because gamma radiation damaged DNA in large numbers of cells at once, stopping cells dividing properly.

Years after the event, survivors shows increase rates of leukemia and other cancers. This is due to gamma radiation causing mutations in cells, which may not show effects immediately but can lead to uncontrolled cell division later.

##### Uses of Gamma radiation - Food irradiation
- Uses gamma radiation to kill harmful microorganisms in food such as bacteria, mould, parasites.
- Gamma rays can pass through the food and damage the DNA of these microorganisms, preventing them from reproducing.
- This process helps them to increase shelf life, prevent food posioning.
- It can treat food after it is packaged, as it has high penetration power.
- Kill microbes throughout food, not just the surface.
- Does not make food radioactive, as it passes through without leaving energy behind.

### Radiation Dose & Risk

Radioactive does not always mean dangerous.

> Radiation risk depends on dose, exposure time, and radiation type.

Low dose: Natural background radiation

Medium dose: Medical X-ray

High dose: Radiation therapy

Very high dose: Nuclear accident

Factors that impact DNA damage:
1. Type of radiation: Different types of radiation have different levels of ionising power and penetration.
2. Exposure time: The longer you are exposed, the more radiation the body absorbs.
3. Inside or outside of body: Radiation inside body is more harmful than radiation than outside.

### How Radiation Causes DNA Damage

Ionising radiation has enough energy to remove electrons from atoms and chemical bonds in DNA.

1. Radiation approaches: A beta particle approaches DNA.
2. Ionisation occurs: Beta particle knocks out an electron from an atom in the DNA backbone.
3. DNA damage: A chemical bond is broken, damaging one strand of DNA.

Damage can lead to:
- Cell malfunction: proteins may be made incorrectly
- Uncontrolled cell growth: Damaged cells may grow and divide abnormally, potentially leading to cancer
- Mutation: Changes in DNA can be passed to daughter cells.
